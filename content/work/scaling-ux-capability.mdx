At a global biotechnology company, a two-person UX team supported an extensive portfolio of internal applications. Many applications were maintained by individual engineers, and years of independent development had resulted in fragmented interfaces, inconsistent user experiences, and limited opportunities for systematic user research.

The organization had established UX modernization as a priority, but the specialist team lacked the capacity to guide every product individually.

I saw an opportunity to approach the problem differently: rather than rely on two specialists to improve every application, **what if we helped engineers develop the skills and practices to make better UX decisions themselves?**

With my manager's support and approximately 10% of my working time allocated to the effort, I volunteered to coordinate an initiative spanning 12 engineering teams across three organizational verticals.

## Build a participation model that teams could sustain

My initial approach involved partnering with product teams to coordinate improvements. An organizational reorganization disrupted that plan, requiring a different way to engage the teams.

Rather than wait for a new organizational structure, I shifted toward a distributed model: one engineering participant from each team, supported by the central UX specialists.

The commitment was intentionally modest:

* One hour per month for shared learning and discussion.
* Two hours per month to apply those practices within each engineer's existing work.

This created a network of engineers who could carry UX knowledge into their teams without requiring substantial new staffing or separate redesign projects.

My responsibility was to coordinate participation, work with engineering leads to accommodate capacity constraints, facilitate learning, and connect engineers with specialist expertise when needed.

The central UX team retained ownership of its professional discipline. I was helping distribute practical capabilities, not replacing UX expertise or claiming ownership of the existing shared design system.

## Make user research accessible to engineers

A central challenge was making UX research practical for engineers who had limited experience conducting it and competing delivery responsibilities.

Working with the UX specialists, I helped develop resources that engineers could use within their normal workflows.

These included a custom GPT that assisted with translating user interviews into jobs-to-be-done (JTBD) statements and personas.

The goal was not to automate UX expertise away. It was to help engineers complete routine research activities more independently, reserving specialist attention for complex questions that required deeper experience.

I also worked with participating engineers and their team leads to identify manageable opportunities for improvement, including legacy design-system upgrades and backlog changes informed by user needs.

Some leads were skeptical about introducing another commitment to already constrained teams. I addressed those concerns by connecting the work to the organization's UX priorities and emphasizing its limited time requirement.

Rather than ask engineers to abandon their roadmaps, the initiative encouraged them to improve the products and workflows they already owned.

## Turn participation into practical adoption

All 12 engineering teams participated in the initiative and produced jobs-to-be-done and persona artifacts.

The work also supported adoption of the organization's existing shared design system. Every participating team either completed an upgrade or established a path toward one.

Beyond initial participation, we saw several encouraging outcomes:

* **Three teams completed their design-system migrations.**
* **Three teams continued conducting UX research independently after the structured initiative.**
* **Three teams engaged the UX specialists for more advanced research or design challenges.**

These groups overlapped and should not be interpreted as nine distinct teams.

More importantly, the initiative began influencing engineering decisions. One team used its research findings to reprioritize backlog work, while another applied user research to inform requirements for a new application.

These examples mattered because the objective was never simply to produce personas or complete training sessions. It was to help teams incorporate user needs into the way they planned and developed software.

The outcomes demonstrated participation, initial design-system adoption, and continued engagement with UX practices. We did not establish a quantitative improvement in end-user satisfaction, and I would not present those outcomes as if we had.

## Scale expertise without centralizing execution

The initiative succeeded through a combination of organizational support and individual participation.

Leadership's prioritization of UX created space for the work. Engineering leads helped make participation feasible. The UX specialists provided professional guidance. Individual engineers brought the practices into their own teams.

My role was to connect those groups and create a workable structure for collaboration.

The experience reinforced an important distinction: **scaling a capability does not necessarily mean centralizing more work or expanding the specialist team.**

Sometimes the more effective approach is to make expertise accessible, lower the cost of participation, and give people the confidence and support to apply new practices independently.

That requires more than distributing tools or instructions. It requires addressing competing priorities, securing participation, maintaining trust in specialist expertise, and ensuring teams can translate learning into their actual work.

The most meaningful outcome was not that 12 teams completed a shared exercise. It was that engineers began incorporating UX research and design-system practices into decisions they already owned.

> **Sustainable organizational change happens when people gain the capability and motivation to improve their own work—not simply when they are instructed to follow a new process.**
