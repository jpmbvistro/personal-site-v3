  - eyebrow: 01 / The challenge
    title: High ambition. Limited specialist capacity.
    layout: prose
    children: |
      A two-person central UX team supported an extensive portfolio of internal applications. Many products were maintained by individual engineers, with fragmented interfaces and limited access to dedicated UX support.

      UX modernization was an organizational priority, but the specialists could not guide every product individually. Engineering leads also had existing delivery commitments. Another large redesign program would have competed with the work teams already owned.

      With my manager's support and approximately **10% of my time**, I volunteered to coordinate participation across **12 engineering teams in three verticals**. The opportunity was to distribute practical capability, not centralize every improvement—or claim authorship of the existing design system.
  - eyebrow: 02 / The approach
    title: Specialist guidance. Local ownership.
    layout: prose
    children: |
      I worked with the UX specialists to make research practical for engineering participants: interviews, jobs-to-be-done (JTBD), personas, and manageable improvements in their own backlogs. A custom GPT helped translate interviews into JTBD and persona artifacts; it did not replace specialist judgment.

      My responsibility was to coordinate participation, work with leads on capacity, facilitate learning, and connect engineers with expertise when needed. The UX specialists retained ownership of their discipline and remained available for complex work.

  - eyebrow: 03 / Key decisions
    title: Adapt the model. Make it easy to say yes.
    layout: decisions
    children: |
      1. **Adapt after reorganization.**
         A reorganization disrupted my original product-team participation plan. I shifted to one engineering representative per team, preserving a route into each team without relying on the former structure. The tradeoff: research needed to be approachable for engineers with limited UX experience.
      2. **Address skepticism with user value.**
         I connected observed user pain points to organizational priorities and a commitment agreed with leads: roughly three hours per engineer per month, including learning and backlog work. Useful, prioritized improvements could fit existing roadmaps rather than compete with them.
      3. **Build capability, not dependency.**
         Resources and specialist partnership helped engineers conduct routine research locally, with an expert path for harder problems. Independence was a direction to support—not an assertion that all 12 teams became self-sufficient.
  - eyebrow: 04 / Results and reflection
    title: Shared participation. Different paths forward.
    layout: prose
    children: |
      All 12 teams participated and produced JTBD and persona artifacts. Every team either adopted the existing design system or established an upgrade path. Follow-through varied, rather than advancing uniformly across the network.

      These groups may overlap; they are not nine distinct teams or additive slices. They show participation, adoption, and continued practice—not a measured improvement in end-user satisfaction.

      The research also informed real planning: one team reprioritized backlog work, while another used research to inform requirements for a new application. The point was not simply to complete artifacts, but to help teams incorporate user needs into decisions they already owned.

      **Organizational sponsorship and embedded ownership were both necessary.** Leadership made room for the work; leads made participation feasible; specialists provided guidance; engineers carried the practices into their teams. My role was to connect those groups and make collaboration workable.

      The lesson I carry forward is to scale capability without making every decision depend on a central team. Lower the cost of participation, maintain trust in expertise, and give people a useful next step in their own work.
