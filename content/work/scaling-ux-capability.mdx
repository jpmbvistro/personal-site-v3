At Moderna, a two-person central UX team supported internal products across departments. Many of those products were built and maintained by individual engineers, and the user experience was fragmented. The challenge was not to redesign one interface myself. It was to help engineering teams make better UX decisions with the specialist capacity we already had.

With management approval and roughly 10% of my time allocated to the effort, I volunteered to coordinate an initiative across 12 engineering teams in three verticals.

## Change the participation model, not the goal

Early participation from product teams was disrupted by a reorganization. Rather than depend on that original structure, I shifted to one engineering participant per team and worked with team leads to keep the commitment manageable: roughly three hours per engineer per month, split between learning and work in their own backlog.

That changed the initiative from a central team doing UX for everyone into a network of engineers who could apply UX practices in their existing work. My role was to coordinate that network, work through constraints with leads, and connect participants with the UX specialists—not to replace those specialists or build the shared design system.

## Make the next improvement practical

I partnered with the UX specialists on tools engineers could use, including a custom GPT to help turn user interviews into jobs-to-be-done and personas. The specialists remained available for complex UX problems; the goal was to make routine research and prioritization more accessible, not suggest that a tool could substitute for their expertise.

Some leads were understandably skeptical of another demand on their teams. I focused the conversation on modest, prioritized improvements tied to users' existing pain points and an organizational objective. Engineers could work on those improvements within their own backlog rather than abandon their roadmap for a separate redesign program.

## Adoption across all 12 teams

All 12 teams participated and created jobs-to-be-done and persona artifacts. Each team either adopted the existing shared design system or established an upgrade path.

These were signs of participation, adoption, and continued practice. They were not a measured improvement in end-user satisfaction, and the overlapping groups should not be added together as distinct teams.

## Enable people rather than compel them

The initiative depended on both executive prioritization and grassroots collaborators. Management support made room for the work, while engineering participants made it relevant to their teams. Neither alone would have been enough.

My practical lesson was to lower the cost of participating, preserve access to specialists, and give teams a useful next step. Sustainable change came from helping people improve the products they already owned—not from compelling everyone to follow a central redesign plan.
