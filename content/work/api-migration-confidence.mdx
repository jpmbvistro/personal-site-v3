      A business-critical proprietary registry and inventory API was being replaced by a new platform and proxy implementation. Internal applications still depended on its behavior.

      When I returned from parental leave, I encountered an inherited endpoint backlog with unclear provenance. Development was underway without reliable behavioral validation.

  - eyebrow: 03 / Key Decisions
    title: When the evidence changed the plan.
    layout: decisions
    intro: |
      I treated the backlog, the client abstraction and one-off testing as assumptions to examine—not conclusions to preserve.
    children: |
      1. **Challenge inherited scope.**
         An inherited backlog could have set the agenda. I asked for usage and support evidence, then worked with Product and engineering leads to agree when work could be retired. Usage verification became part of migration planning rather than assuming every ticket was necessary.
      2. **Abandon an unreliable abstraction.**
         Typed definitions looked authoritative but did not reliably reflect legacy behavior. I deprioritized static comparison rather than expand an untrustworthy check, redirecting the investigation toward live responses and implementation logic.
      3. **Make validation repeatable.**
         A one-off investigation was not a delivery capability. Shadow-diff checks were integrated into CI, and engineering leadership prioritized corrective bugs other than negative-path parity issues. Repeatable validation did not make identified issues equivalent to resolved defects.
  - eyebrow: 04 / Results and Reflection
    title: Less unnecessary work. Better evidence of correctness.
    layout: prose
    intro: |
      The initial static comparison had limited value because its abstraction did not reliably describe actual behavior. I would establish that source of truth earlier and keep the MVP tighter.

      I would also involve engineers earlier in investigating discrepancies, making difficult findings a shared discovery and giving colleagues a role in validating the assumptions behind their work.
