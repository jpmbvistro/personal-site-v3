Through Impact Innovations, I worked on a technology decision for a resource-constrained nonprofit that needed CRM and fundraising capabilities without ongoing monthly platform-fee risk. Both contenders offered free monthly tiers, but both charged per-transaction fees. A free subscription did not make their long-term economics equivalent.

The lower-transaction-fee platform lacked one required feature at its free tier. Paying for an upgrade was outside the nonprofit's budget. The higher-fee competitor met the immediate functional need, but was a worse long-term fit for a maturing development operation. Accepting that apparent tradeoff would have solved the feature problem while committing the client to higher transaction costs.

## Model the economics of a growing donor base

I created a configurable donor-pyramid financial model to make the longer-term cost visible. It represented donor share across giving tiers, expected donor growth, and transactions over time, then projected cumulative transaction fees under the competing platforms.

The model made it possible to explore how the donor mix and fundraising activity affected the decision, rather than compare monthly subscription prices alone. It revealed a material projected cost difference in favor of the lower-fee platform.

Those figures were projections based on assumptions, not savings already realized. Their purpose was to inform a choice before migration, not present a forecast as a measured client outcome.

## Change what the client could choose

The decision was not only about ranking two fixed feature lists. The lower-fee vendor's business objectives aligned with serving this client profile, creating a basis for a commercial conversation about the single missing feature.

Impact Innovations negotiated with that vendor, which enabled the feature without requiring the unaffordable paid tier. That changed the feasible options: the nonprofit could meet its required functionality while retaining the lower transaction-fee structure and avoiding ongoing monthly platform fees.

The cost model helped explain why the difference mattered; vendor partnership made the preferable option usable. Neither technical fit nor financial modeling alone would have resolved the original constraint.

## Follow the decision through adoption

The client selected the lower-fee solution, fully migrated, integrated it into the existing stack, and used it to run later fundraising events. The engagement therefore went beyond a recommendation: the selected platform became part of the nonprofit's actual fundraising operation.

Actual first-year fundraising data confirmed significant transaction-fee savings versus the alternative platform. That comparison used realized fundraising activity, distinct from the earlier donor-growth projections. The client identity and exact fundraising, fee, and savings amounts remain confidential.

The engagement also strengthened Impact Innovations' relationships with the client and vendor. The outcome depended on choosing a workable platform, establishing a sustainable commercial fit, and completing adoption—not simply delivering an integration.

## Technology strategy includes the available deal

The lesson I took from this engagement was to test whether an apparent cost-versus-capability tradeoff is actually fixed. A model can make the consequences of a decision visible, and a vendor relationship can change the decision itself.

Responsibility for the client outcome meant carrying that reasoning through migration and use, then checking the economics against actual activity. The right choice was the one the nonprofit could afford, adopt, and continue using as its fundraising operation matured.
