Through Impact Innovations, I helped a resource-constrained nonprofit select a CRM and fundraising platform that could support its immediate needs without becoming financially burdensome as its donor program grew.

Two platforms were under consideration. Both offered free monthly subscription tiers, but their transaction fees and included capabilities differed.

The lower-transaction-fee platform lacked one essential feature unless the organization purchased an upgrade it could not afford. The competing platform included the feature but charged higher transaction fees and offered a less attractive path for the nonprofit's longer-term development needs.

**Neither option was ideal as offered.**

Rather than treating the vendor offerings as fixed, I approached the decision from two directions: understand the financial implications of each platform, then explore whether the more affordable option could meet the nonprofit's actual requirements.

## Model the economics beyond subscription pricing

Comparing the platforms' monthly subscription costs would have obscured their most consequential financial difference.

I developed a configurable donor-pyramid model that represented the nonprofit's fundraising activity across different giving tiers. The model accounted for the distribution of donors, anticipated growth, and transaction volume to project cumulative processing fees under each platform.

This allowed us to examine how different donor mixes and growth assumptions would affect the cost of operating each system over time.

The analysis demonstrated a meaningful projected cost advantage for the lower-transaction-fee platform.

These were scenario-based projections, not realized savings. Their value was in making the longer-term consequences of the decision visible before the nonprofit committed to a platform and migrated its data.

The analysis also reframed the question. We were no longer simply comparing two free subscription tiers. We were evaluating the financial sustainability of the nonprofit's fundraising infrastructure.

## Negotiate a better option instead of accepting the tradeoff

The financial model established which platform offered more favorable economics, but it did not resolve the missing-feature problem.

The lower-fee vendor was interested in serving organizations with this nonprofit's profile, creating an opportunity to explore a mutually beneficial arrangement.

Impact Innovations negotiated with the vendor, which agreed to make the required capability available without forcing the nonprofit onto an unaffordable paid subscription tier.

**That negotiation changed the decision itself.**

Instead of choosing between a platform with the necessary functionality and one with better economics, the nonprofit now had an option that met both requirements.

The cost model demonstrated why the difference mattered. The vendor relationship made the financially preferable solution practical.

This was not simply a matter of identifying the best existing product. It required recognizing that the commercial terms were part of the solution and could be influenced.

## Follow the decision through implementation and adoption

The nonprofit selected the lower-fee platform, completed its CRM migration, and integrated the system into its existing technology stack.

The organization subsequently used the platform to support fundraising events, demonstrating that the solution worked beyond the initial evaluation and selection process.

After the first year, we compared actual fundraising activity against the alternative platform's transaction-fee structure.

**The realized activity confirmed significant transaction-fee savings relative to the competing option.**

That finding was distinct from the original growth projections. It evaluated the platform decision against actual first-year fundraising transactions rather than assumed future donor behavior.

The exact financial results and client identity remain confidential.

The engagement also strengthened relationships between Impact Innovations, the nonprofit, and the vendor. The outcome depended on more than selecting software: it required financial analysis, commercial negotiation, technical implementation, and successful adoption.

## Technology strategy includes shaping the available choices

The most important lesson from this engagement was that technology decisions do not always have to be made within the options initially presented.

A conventional comparison might have concluded that the nonprofit needed to accept either higher processing costs or an unaffordable subscription to obtain its required functionality.

By modeling the economics and exploring the vendor's interests, we challenged that assumption and created a third possibility.

The experience also reinforced the importance of carrying technology decisions through implementation and evaluating their actual results. A financially attractive platform is not a successful solution if an organization cannot adopt it or use it effectively.

> Good technology strategy is not simply choosing the best available tool. It is understanding the organization's constraints, identifying what can be changed, and creating a solution that remains workable over time.
