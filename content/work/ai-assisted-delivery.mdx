  - eyebrow: 03 / Key Decisions
    title: Increase throughput without losing control.
    layout: decisions
    intro: |
      The architecture evolved during delivery. I invested in leverage, proved new capabilities incrementally and used observed failures to decide which controls actually helped.
    children: |
      1. **Invest in the system while meeting the deadline.**
         Building the harness consumed time that could have gone directly to tickets. I reduced that risk by reusing earlier architecture and behavioral tests, then completing actual work as I developed the new system. Automation was part of the delivery strategy, not a separate project waiting for the backlog to stop.
      2. **Make parallel execution independent and verifiable.**
         I designed for concurrency from the beginning but proved sequential execution first. One Git worktree and isolated test context per ticket reduced interference and allowed independent evaluation. Once sequential delivery demonstrated useful reliability and throughput, I enabled parallel execution during the same delivery window.
      3. **Use deterministic controls where certainty is possible.**
         Stronger prompts were a poor substitute for Codex's deterministic structured-output interface. Rigid keyword-based scope checks also struggled to interpret intent. I replaced the first with structured outputs and the second with agent judgment, retaining deterministic validation for objective requirements. Both corrections happened during the 30-day period.
      4. **Engineer recovery instead of assuming perfect autonomy.**
         Bounded retries distinguished potential transient failures from persistent problems. Persistent failures triggered human handoff and ad hoc agent assistance. Langfuse evidence supported debugging-agent investigations and targeted harness improvements. The objective was dependable throughput with a recovery path—not unattended autonomy.

      ### What changed in my engineering approach

      In this organizational context, explicit controls helped establish confidence in an unfamiliar delivery model. But excessive deterministic checks became brittle when the underlying decision required interpreting intent.

      My subsequent harness designs became thinner: preserve strict validation for genuinely verifiable requirements, while allowing agents more flexibility in implementation and reasoning. This later philosophy was a result of the experience, not the architecture I started with.
