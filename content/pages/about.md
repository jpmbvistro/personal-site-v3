    paragraphs: |
      My journey hasn't followed a conventional engineering leadership path.

      I started working in software development in 2012, building applications and learning the foundations of engineering. But some of my most formative leadership experiences came from outside technology organizations.

      At EMW Community Space in Cambridge, I helped direct nonprofit programs and co-led East Meets Words, an Asian-American open mic that brought people together through storytelling, art, and community.

      That work taught me lessons I still rely on: how to build trust, bring people together around shared goals, navigate competing needs, and create environments where people feel ownership over what they're building.

      Those lessons became increasingly relevant as my engineering career progressed.

      I went on to develop customer-facing software at DraftKings and later worked on internal platforms, developer tooling, and engineering systems in the life sciences industry.

      There, my responsibilities expanded beyond writing software.

      I coordinated a distributed UX initiative involving 12 engineering teams, challenged assumptions behind a major API migration, and developed AI-assisted engineering systems to help teams investigate and deliver complex work more reliably.

      I also mentored engineers, participated in hiring, and took on interim engineering-management responsibilities during organizational transitions.

      Across these experiences, I learned that technical architecture and organizational design have something important in common: **both need to work for the people who depend on them.**

I'm Juan Vistro, a software engineer and technical leader whose work spans product engineering, developer platforms, nonprofit leadership, and community-centered technology.

I care about what happens beyond the implementation: whether people understand a system, trust it, can maintain it, and have the ability to improve it themselves.

My career has taken me through different industries and responsibilities, but the central question has remained surprisingly consistent:

**How do we build systems that make the people using them more capable?**
